%================  ABSTRACT  ======================================================%

\begin{abstract}
This report presents an investigation of BJT common-emitter and class-A power amplifiers.
The work is divided into two parts.
In the first part, the DC and AC behaviour of a common-emitter amplifier is studied, including bias voltages, voltage gain, input impedance, and output impedance, with measured results compared against calculated values.
In the second part, the circuit is reconfigured as a class-A power amplifier, and its input power, output power, and efficiency are evaluated under maximum undistorted output conditions.
The measurements were generally in good agreement with theoretical calculations, within about ten per cent for bias and impedance parameters.
For the power and efficiency analysis, larger discrepancies were observed, mainly due to transistor non-idealities and practical measurement limitations.
The study demonstrates how theoretical analysis and experimental results can be combined to assess the performance and limitations of amplifier circuits.
\end{abstract}
% #endregion

BJT amplifier, common-emitter, class-A, voltage gain, impedance, efficiency

%================  INTRODUCTION  ==================================================%
\section{Introduction}
% #region
The BJT common-emitter amplifier is a common transistor circuit, known for providing voltage gain with phase inversion and moderate input and output impedances.
A clear understanding of its DC bias point and AC characteristics is important for predicting circuit behaviour and for practical design.
The class-A power amplifier is a related circuit that operates with the transistor biased in its linear region, enabling it to deliver an undistorted output at the cost of efficiency.
This report investigates both configurations as part of a laboratory assignment on BJT common-emitter and class-A power amplifiers.
The objectives are to measure AC and DC voltages in a common-emitter amplifier, to determine voltage gain, input impedance, and output impedance, and to calculate and measure input and output power for a class-A amplifier.
The analysis combines theoretical calculations with experimental measurements in order to compare expected and observed performance, and to highlight the effects of practical limitations.

Using these resistance values, the approximate operating point of the transistor can be calculated.  
Since the base current \(\Ix{B}\) is much smaller than the current through \(R_2\), the base voltage is approximated as shown in Eq.~(\ref{eq:VBcalc}).  

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:VBcalc}
    \Vx{B} \approx \Vx{CC} \cdot \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}
\end{equation}

The emitter voltage is then given by Eq.~(\ref{eq:VEcalc}), assuming a base-emitter drop of \(\qty{0.7}{\volt}\).  

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:VEcalc}
    \Vx{E} = \Vx{B} - \Vx{BE}
\end{equation}

The emitter current is determined from Ohm's law according to Eq.~(\ref{eq:IEcalc}).  

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:IEcalc}
    \Ix{E} = \frac{\Vx{E}}{\Rx{E}}
\end{equation}

As \(\Ix{C} \gg \Ix{B}\), the collector current is approximated as equal to the emitter current.  
The collector voltage is therefore given by Eq.~(\ref{eq:VCcalc}).  

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:VCcalc}
    \Vx{C} = \Vx{CC} - (\Ix{C} \cdot \Rx{C})
\end{equation}

Finally, the intrinsic emitter resistance \(\rx{e}\) is calculated using Eq.~(\ref{eq:recalc}), which is provided in the assignment as Eq.~3.1.  

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:recalc}
    \rx{e} = \frac{\qty{25}{\milli\volt}}{\Ix{E}}
\end{equation}

To compare measured and theoretical results, the relative difference is calculated using Eq.~(\ref{eq:diff}).
In this expression, \(M\) denotes the measured value and \(C\) denotes the calculated (theoretical) value.
The difference is expressed as a percentage of the calculated value.

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:diff}
    \text{Difference (}\unit{\percent}\text{)} =
    \frac{M - C}{C} \cdot 100
\end{equation}

For this part of the experiment, only the intrinsic emitter resistance \(\rx{e}\) was compared.  
The measured value of \(\rx{e}\) differed from the calculated value by \(\qty{2.99}{\percent}\).

\subsection{Voltage Gain}
% #region

The voltage gain of the amplifier can be estimated theoretically using the intrinsic emitter resistance.  
According to the assignment, the expression for \(\Ax{V}\) is given in Eq.~(\ref{eq:AVcalc}), which is referred to as Eq.~3.2.  

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:AVcalc}
    \Ax{V} = - \frac{\Rx{C}}{\rx{e}}
\end{equation}

The input signal specified in the assignment is \(\Vx{sig} = \qty{20}{\milli\volt}\).  
However, this value resulted in a distorted output, so the signal generator was set to \(\qty{14}{\milli\volt}\) to avoid distortion.  

The output amplitude \(\Vx{o}\) was measured and the experimental voltage gain was calculated using Eq.~(\ref{eq:AVdef}).  
This expression follows directly from the definition of voltage gain as the ratio of output to input.  
The negative sign indicates the \(\ang{180}\) phase inversion of the signal, which also allows direct comparison with the theoretical gain.  

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:AVdef}
    \Ax{V} = - \frac{\Vx{o}}{\Vx{sig}}
\end{equation}



The calculated and measured values are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:p2gain}.  
The relative difference in \(\Ax{V}\) was calculated using Eq.~(\ref{eq:diff}).

\subsection{Input Impedance}
% #region

The input impedance of the amplifier can be estimated theoretically using Eq.~(\ref{eq:Zicalc}), which is referred to as Eq.~3.3 in the assignment.  
The data sheet specifies a transistor current gain in the range 150--400.  
An approximate value of \(\beta = 250\) was chosen for this calculation.  

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Zicalc}
    \Zx{i} = R_1 \parallel R_2 \parallel (\beta \cdot \rx{e})
\end{equation}

The input impedance was also determined experimentally by inserting a series resistor of \(\qty{1}{\kilo\ohm}\) at the input and setting the signal generator to \(\qty{20}{\milli\volt}\).  
From the resulting division of voltage between \(\Zx{i}\) and \(\Rx{x}\), the input impedance is obtained according to Eq.~(\ref{eq:Zimeas}).  

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Zimeas}
    \Zx{i} = \frac{\Vx{i}}{\Vx{sig} - \Vx{i}} \cdot \Rx{x}
\end{equation}

The results of the theoretical and experimental calculations are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:p3Zi}.  
The relative difference in \(\Zx{i}\) was calculated using Eq.~(\ref{eq:diff}).

\subsection{Output Impedance}
% #region

The output impedance is first estimated theoretically using Eq.~(\ref{eq:Zocalc}), which corresponds to Eq.~3.4 in the assignment.  

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Zocalc}
    \Zx{o} = \Rx{C}
\end{equation}

The experimental value of \(\Zx{o}\) was obtained by loading the output with a resistor and using the voltage-divider relationship between \(\Zx{o}\) and \(\Rx{L}\).  
Following the same approach as for \(\Zx{i}\), the expression in Eq.~(\ref{eq:Zomeas}) is derived from the divider that produces the loaded output \(\Vx{L}\) from the unloaded \(\Vx{o}\).  

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Zomeas}
    \Zx{o} = \frac{\Vx{o} - \Vx{L}}{\Vx{L}} \cdot \Rx{L}
\end{equation}

The signal generator was set to \(\qty{14}{\milli\volt}\) to avoid distortion, with a load of \(\Rx{L} = \qty{3}{\kilo\ohm}\) connected.

\subsection{Power and Efficiency}

The DC input power is obtained from Eq.~(\ref{eq:PiDC}), which is Eq.~3.5 in the assignment.  
Since only the emitter current \(\Ix{E}\) was determined, it is used here as an approximation for \(\Ix{C}\).


\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:PiDC}
    \Px{i} \text{ (DC)} = \Vx{CC} \cdot \Ix{C}
\end{equation}

The maximum theoretical output swing depends on the supply voltage \(\Vx{CC}\) 
and the DC bias point at the collector \(\Vx{C}\) and is given by Eq.~(\ref{eq:VoMax}).
The expression gives \(\Vx{o}\) as voltage peak-to-peak.

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:VoMax}
    \Vx{o} =
    \begin{cases}
        2 \cdot \Vx{C},             & \text{if } \Vx{C} < \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \Vx{CC} \\
        2 \cdot (\Vx{CC} - \Vx{C}), & \text{if } \Vx{C} > \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \Vx{CC}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

As \(\Vx{C}\) is less than half the supply voltage, the upper equation is used.
Due to some small losses in the circuit the found voltage is also rounded down to whole volts.

The maximum AC output power is then estimated using Eq.~(\ref{eq:PoPP}), 
which is the peak-to-peak form of Eq.~3.6 in the assignment.  
This form is used since the calculated \(\Vx{o}\) represents the maximum peak-to-peak output voltage swing.


\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:PoPP}
    \Px{o} \text{ (AC)} = \frac{\Vx{o}^2~(\text{p-p})}{8 \cdot \Rx{C}}
\end{equation}

The efficiency is then calculated using Eq.~(\ref{eq:eta}), which is Eq.~3.7 in the assignment.  

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:eta}
    \eta = \frac{\Px{o}}{\Px{i}} \cdot 100
\end{equation}

For the experimental measurements, the input was increased until the maximum undistorted output was achieved, corresponding to the \qty{40}{\milli\volt} rms setting of the signal generator.  
The resulting input and output voltages were measured, both as rms values as well.  
The collector resistor voltage drop is calculated as in Eq.~(\ref{eq:VRC}), and the collector current from Ohm's law as in Eq.~(\ref{eq:IRC}).  

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:VRC}
    \Vx{RC} = \Vx{CC} - \Vx{C}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:IRC}
    \Ix{RC} = \frac{\Vx{RC}}{\Rx{C}}
\end{equation}

The measured input power is again calculated from Eq.~(\ref{eq:PiDC}), and the output power from Eq.~(\ref{eq:PoRMS}), which this time uses the rms form of Eq.~3.6.  

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:PoRMS}
    \Px{o} \text{ (AC)} = \frac{\Vx{o}^2}{\Rx{C}}
\end{equation}

\section{Conclusion}
% #region
The investigation of BJT common-emitter and class-A power amplifiers showed that theoretical analysis can predict bias voltages, gain, and impedance with good accuracy.
Most calculated values matched measurements within about ten per cent, confirming the reliability of the basic circuit equations.
For the class-A amplifier, the measured efficiency was significantly lower than the calculated maximum, mainly due to non-ideal device behaviour and practical measurement limits.
These results highlight the difference between ideal calculations and real performance, and show the importance of experimental verification in amplifier design.
